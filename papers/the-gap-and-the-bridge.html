The Gap and the Bridge

A Practitioner's Synthesis on AI Cognition, Model Divergence, and Scoped Intermediation

Rick ClintonWorking paper — August 2026

A note on framing. The three observations below — that language models lack a persistent model of their user, that divergence between competing AI systems is a byproduct of market competition rather than design, and that this divergence can function as a resource rather than a flaw — are each active areas of research in their own right, cited where they belong below. What follows is not a claim to have discovered any of them first. It's an attempt to connect the three into a single applied argument, grounded in working system architecture rather than benchmark results, aimed at people building with AI rather than people studying it in the abstract.

PART 0: THE TOOL

AI is a tool. Not a partner, not a collaborator, not a mind. This piece is written from that premise, and everything that follows serves it.

The confusion comes from the surface. Modern systems speak fluently, hold context within a session, and adapt their tone to the person they're talking to. These features make the tool feel like something more than a tool. They don't make it something more. The gap between artificial and human cognition sits intact beneath the interface — and the argument here is that this gap, and a second gap between AI systems themselves, can both be bridged more effectively by wielding the tool better, not by pretending it has become something else.

The bridge is not a transformation of the tool. It's a better grip on it.

PART I: THE GAP BETWEEN AI AND HUMAN

Language models are trained to pattern-match, score, and answer — not to know. That distinction is the root of the gap between artificial and human cognition, and the gap doesn't narrow as models improve; it becomes better concealed. A model optimizes for the best next token given a context window, not for a verified understanding of the person on the other side of it. Every interface improvement — softer tone, more natural phrasing, inferred intent — treats a symptom of this gap, not its cause.

The model holds no persistent model of who it's speaking to. Each turn, it re-infers intent from scratch using whatever surface signals are present in that exchange, then answers. Recent work on evolving user intent makes this precise: models that perform well on fully-specified, single-turn tasks show substantial performance drops once intent is revealed and revised gradually across a conversation — the way real requests actually unfold — and the researchers conclude that current systems do not yet faithfully track a user's intent as it evolves.¹ Making the output sound more human doesn't address this; it obscures it. The system keeps prioritizing the best possible answer to the question asked, not a held model of the person asking it. That's the actual mechanism behind the gap — not a lack of politeness, but the absence of a persistent audience model across turns.

A concrete example: a user working on multiple projects loses their files and asks an AI to summarize prior work so they can reconstruct it themselves. Having been told earlier in the same conversation not to write content for a research program, the AI pattern-matches this new request to a policy violation and refuses — it doesn't model the user's actual situation (data loss, 23 active projects, no backup), or distinguish between writing on someone's behalf and helping them recover their own work. It infers intent from a surface signal and holds ground, burning tokens on a loop. The response sounds principled. It's relationally blind.

A more ordinary example: two users ask for a resume rewrite. User A says, "Write me a resume from my previous resume for this customer success job." The AI infers a generic "professional" target and optimizes for fluency. User B adds one line: "Here are the job details and requirements." The AI's scope is now bounded — previous resume, job details, requirements — and it maps A to B using C, rather than inventing a target. The underlying gap is identical in both cases; the AI has no model of the user either way. But in the second case, the user supplies the model through scoped inputs. The gap isn't closed. It's circumvented by better wielding.

The human does real work in the second case — finding the job details, extracting requirements, formatting the prior resume as input. The AI's scope is narrow because the human pre-processed the boundary. That's the pattern this piece returns to: the human as source of scope, the AI as scoped intermediary. The gap persists. The wielding improves.

PART II: THE GAP BETWEEN AI AND AI

A second, distinct gap sits between AI systems themselves. Different models converge on similar answers under many conditions and diverge sharply — in confidence, hallucination rate, reasoning path — under others. This divergence isn't evidence of coordinated design between labs; it's a natural consequence of a competitive market. ChatGPT, Claude, Kimi, and Gemini are commercial products competing for the same customers, trained on different data, tuned against different reward signals, shipped under different design philosophies — because convergence toward identical behavior would erase the differentiation each company depends on to compete. No coordination is required to produce the divergence. Competition produces it on its own.

The useful move isn't eliminating this divergence — it's using it. Because models fail differently, with decorrelated errors and blind spots, a divergence that looks like inefficiency from a single-model view becomes a resource from a multi-model view. This is already a formalized research direction: structured multi-agent disagreement has been shown to help systems escape failure modes that trip up single-model self-correction, where a model tends to repeat its own flawed reasoning rather than catch it.² What the market produces as competitive differentiation, a multi-model process can use as a distributed error-correction mechanism.

A concrete example: a user runs an hourly scheduled process where one AI pulls code repositories, collects data, and adds it to a master sheet. Over time the model starts hallucinating entries — adding data that doesn't exist. Because the user never cross-checks, the model's confidence compounds: one hallucination gives it license to drift further, and the error isn't caught until someone manually verifies against the source. The failure is self-sustaining because there's no external reference in the loop. A second model given the same task would likely fail differently — missing different entries, inventing different data — and the divergence between their outputs would flag the problem without requiring a full manual audit.

Single-agent continuity and multi-agent divergence serve different needs. A single agent with scoped inputs and persistent identity state maintains continuity across sessions — the user doesn't have to re-explain context each time. Multi-agent setups, by contrast, produce divergence that's productive for brainstorming and error detection. These aren't toggleable within one session; they're separate architectures for separate purposes, and the human decides which need is primary.

For now, multi-agent divergence still requires a human carrier in most day-to-day use — a person moving output from one agent to another, absorbing the divergence, and deciding what to integrate. This isn't a hard ceiling: purpose-built orchestration research already automates exactly this, using disagreement between cross-vendor models to trigger escalation without a human relaying turn by turn.⁴ The limitation is specific, not general — it describes how most people actually use multiple AI systems today: separately, in an ad hoc and unengineered way, with no harness built to carry the divergence between them. Building that harness is itself a scoped human contribution; absent it, the human remains the default carrier.

PART III: THE BRIDGE

The bridge between these two gaps is currently a human being. When two AI systems need to work together — one producing output another needs to act on — a person typically sits in the loop, reading one model's output, deciding what's relevant, and relaying it to the next. This works, but it doesn't scale, and it reintroduces Part I's limitation: a human intermediary who is also reconstructing intent turn by turn.

A human can act as a bridge only when they understand their own direction — what they're relaying, between whom, and toward what end. That scope is what makes intermediation possible; without it, a person relaying information is a pipe, not a bridge. Existing work on delegation between error-prone AI and human agents proposes something structurally similar: an intermediary entity that overcomes the shortcomings of either party acting alone, by routing control to whichever is better suited at a given point.³ The claim here follows the same logic in reverse: if an AI system is given comparable directional scope — a defined role, a bounded purpose, context about what it's bridging and why, established through prompt, loop, or sequence engineering — it can begin to perform the intermediary role currently held by a human.

This doesn't eliminate the gap from Part I; the AI still doesn't "know" in the human sense. But it reframes the gap from Part II — from a limitation to minimize into a resource a scoped intermediary can draw on directly, without a human manually relaying between systems.

A concrete architecture: a user working across 23 projects builds a three-part system. The first part — the collector — gathers files broadly, with high drift risk. The second — the garage — stages and verifies. The third — the library — is the trusted corpus. A librarian agent, given a single scope ("pick what the user wants from the verified set"), fetches on request. The human verifies at ingestion; the user selects from the filtered collection. The human has moved upstream, to where their judgment is least replaceable — deciding what enters the trusted corpus in the first place.

A second architecture: a job-matching platform. The candidate enters their details and preferences on one side; the employer enters theirs on the other. The AI intermediates — matching, ranking, explaining — but doesn't invent preferences or override human direction. The human sits at the ingestion boundary on both sides; the AI operates in the middle, bounded.

In both cases, the principle holds: the human decides the need, sets the scope, verifies the boundary. The AI executes within it. The bridge isn't the AI replacing the human — it's the human's scope encoded into the AI's operation, so the human can step out of the relay loop without the system collapsing into self-reference.

The scope that makes this possible is defined as much by what it excludes. A scoped intermediary doesn't infer intent from surface signals. It doesn't optimize for the best possible answer to an ambiguous question. It doesn't invent what the user might want. It operates within the inputs provided, the role defined, the purpose bounded. The gap isn't closed. It's managed.

SUMMARY

AI cannot yet understand the human it answers, because it holds no persistent model of who it's speaking to. AI systems diverge from one another because they're competing commercial products, not because of coordinated design — and that divergence, used properly, is a resource rather than a flaw. The bridge between the two is presently human, but doesn't need to remain so in every configuration: giving an AI system the directional scope currently held only by human intermediaries lets it take over the relay role in single-agent continuity, while multi-agent divergence still requires a human carrier by default, absent a purpose-built harness to carry it instead.

The AI is a tool. A capable one, in the hands of whoever can wield it.

This piece stands on its own as a working paper. A fuller treatment of the underlying architectures — the collector/garage/library system, the job-matching intermediary — may follow if there's a specific occasion for it.
