JUDGE

A Five-Layer Stack for Deterministic External Verification of LLM Claims

Rick Clinton — 2026

ABSTRACT

AI systems are increasingly asked to report whether their own outputs are correct, safe, or complete. We argue this is the wrong place to locate the final decision — a model's explanation, confidence, or self-critique is generated by the same probabilistic process whose behavior is under review. JUDGE proposes a five-layer architecture that converts model behavior into explicit claims and subjects those claims to deterministic, externally replayable gates before an action is accepted. The layers are: claim and intent contracts, evidence normalization, semantic checks, trust-boundary enforcement, and review with audit and abstention.

JUDGE is a position and experimental agenda, not a completed end-to-end system. Its feasibility is motivated by limited component-level evidence from six supporting prototypes. These artifacts have exposed real implementation defects under tests and fault injection, but they do not yet validate the integrated stack or establish broad generalization. Our central claim is deliberately narrow: whenever a requirement can be stated as an executable invariant over available evidence, acceptance should depend on that invariant rather than on the model's assertion that it complied.

1. Position: self-report is evidence, not authority

Large language models can critique answers, verbalize confidence, and serve as judges of other models' outputs. These capabilities are operationally attractive because they scale beyond human review — but they conflate two roles: producing a candidate and deciding whether the candidate may pass. Model-based evaluation remains sensitive to prompt phrasing, presentation, correlated errors, and the evaluator's own learned preferences.

The position: for requirements that can be expressed as executable invariants over available evidence, a probabilistic model may propose and explain, but a deterministic external mechanism should decide whether the requirement is satisfied. This is not the claim that all semantic correctness is decidable, or that model self-evaluation has no value — many judgments remain underspecified or irreducibly social. The claim concerns a smaller, important subset: schema validity, provenance requirements, authorization boundaries, graph consistency, contradiction rules, required review, and fail-closed behavior under missing evidence.

The verifier must operate outside the model's conversational context, consume explicit inputs, produce reproducible decisions, and expose the rule and evidence that determined the outcome. If the evidence is insufficient, the correct result is not an invented probability of safety but an abstention or escalation.

2. The proposed five-layer stack

JUDGE places five gates between model output and consequential execution. The stack is a proposed integration; the individual prototypes are not yet an end-to-end implementation.

LayerResponsibilityFailure disposition
1. Claim & intent contractBind action, facts, actor, scope, and expected outcome to a typed representationReject malformed or unbound claims
2. Evidence normalizationResolve evidence into canonical identifiers and provenance without silently repairing ambiguityAbstain on missing/ambiguous/conflicting evidence
3. Semantic checksApply explicit rules to the claim–evidence graph: consistency, entailment, cycles, forbidden statesRule-specific pass / fail / unknown
4. Trust-boundary enforcementConfirm only authorized principals, tools, and data may cause the requested state transitionDeny by default; record the violated boundary
5. Review, audit, escalationRoute unresolved or high-impact cases to independent review; preserve inputs and rules for replayQuarantine, request evidence, or require human approval

A later layer cannot convert an earlier failure into a pass merely because a model supplies a persuasive explanation. The decision function is three-valued — accept, reject, or abstain — because forcing uncertainty into a Boolean pass/fail can reward silent assumptions. Models can still translate natural language into candidate claims or suggest rules; those products remain untrusted until contract checks and deterministic rules accept them.

3. Preliminary component evidence — and its limits

Several prototypes motivate feasibility, but none establishes the paper's end-to-end thesis. We report their status to define what exists and to prevent design specifications from being mistaken for results.

  • Deterministic reasoning kernel — an eight-rule integrity engine achieved 16/16 accuracy and 8/8 intended honest abstentions. Testing exposed and led to correction of a cycle-detection defect involving SUPPORTS edges. Too small and component-specific to imply general reasoning reliability.
  • Intent graph — on 15 synthetic trajectories, detected all targeted cases with no false positives. The sample is small and synthetic; the numbers describe only those 15 trajectories, not expected deployment performance.
  • Trust boundary & review board — integration testing found and fixed real defects; fault injection exercised failure paths. Not a comparative safety evaluation, and doesn't quantify coverage of unknown failure modes.
  • One mechanism (on hold) — produced an inverted result (AUC 0.332) and is excluded from positive evidence. Its failure reinforces the paper's methodological commitment: a mechanism should not gain authority from its intended role or a plausible narrative; it must pass independently specified tests.

Collectively, these results show deterministic gates can be implemented, can abstain, and can uncover defects in their own logic and integrations. They do not show that the proposed layers compose correctly, that rules cover real-world semantics, or that JUDGE outperforms self-report or model-based judging.

4. Proposed experiments (not yet run)

A preregistered evaluation isolating verifier correctness from upstream claim construction:

  • E1 — canonical-input verifier evaluation. Compare self-report, a separate LLM judge, deterministic JUDGE rules, and a hybrid, on a benchmark spanning schema, provenance, authorization, contradiction, cycle, and missing-evidence cases.
  • E2 — end-to-end contract evaluation. Pass raw model outputs through the full pipeline and attribute each failure to its first invalid state, so a strong kernel score can't hide translation or integration failures.
  • E3 — correlated-error stress test. Create cases where the generator and an LLM judge share misleading wording or a shared incorrect premise; measure whether judge errors correlate with generator errors while deterministic rules remain invariant.
  • E4 — mutation and fault injection. Mutate each layer — omit provenance, inject cycles, cross an authorization boundary — and measure mutation score, fail-closed rate, detection latency, and audit completeness.
  • E5 — rule adequacy and operational cost. Measure the fraction of requirements expressible as deterministic invariants, rule-authoring time, runtime overhead, and reviewer load, across at least two deployment domains.

All negative findings — low coverage, inverted predictors, integration regressions — should be retained.

5. Limitations, failure modes, and societal impact

"Deterministic" does not mean "correct." A deterministic verifier can faithfully repeat a bad rule. JUDGE requires rule tests, mutation testing, explicit versioning, and audit replay — determinism supplies inspectability and reproducibility, not truth by fiat.

Natural language cannot always be reduced to invariants; JUDGE should abstain when claim binding is ambiguous. The model may corrupt the structured claim before verification — this is a central threat, not an edge case, which is why the canonical-input and end-to-end experiments are kept separate. Rule coverage can create a false sense of security: passing all encoded checks establishes only that encoded checks passed. A team could add many narrow, easy checks, report a high pass rate, and leave the consequential risk surface unmodeled.

Societal impact depends on deployment. External gates could reduce unsafe or unauthorized model actions and make consequential decisions more auditable. Conversely, a flawed or selectively designed rule set could legitimize harmful decisions, encode institutional bias, or shift accountability from deployers to an apparently objective mechanism. Human appeal paths, risk-weighted coverage audits, and disclosure of unverified properties are necessary safeguards, not optional presentation details.

6. Conclusion

AI systems should not be the final authority on whether their own outputs satisfy hard requirements. JUDGE proposes a five-layer separation between probabilistic proposal and deterministic acceptance. Existing prototypes provide limited feasibility evidence and useful negative lessons — not validation of the integrated architecture. The immediate research task is empirical and falsifiable: determine which requirements can be encoded, how often the full pipeline fails, what abstention costs, and whether external gates reduce consequential false acceptance without concealing coverage gaps. Until those experiments are run, JUDGE should be read as a position and a testable design — not a demonstrated solution.
